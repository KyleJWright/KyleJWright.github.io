Mathematical physicist working on AI safety and alignment · London

My Background

I’m a mathematical physicist by training — I did my PhD in theoretical physics at the Australian National University and then held a postdoctoral position at NYU Abu Dhabi. I spent my academic career studying the geometry and topology of string theory, particularly anything related to T-duality. I was driven by a desire to understand what the universe would look like from the perspective of a stringy probe. A partial answer is given by things like generalized geometry and \(L_\infty\)-algebroids (or better yet \(L_\infty\)-groupoids). Lots of fun mathematics, but the impact was difficult to see and explain.

I have also spent some time working in industry. I have worked as a consultant doing meteorological and dispersion modelling to predict the impact of proposed mining projects. The impact was clear: I was working to ensure ground-level concentrations of harmful chemicals (things like NO\(_x\), SO\(_x\) and other small particulate matter) stayed within safe limits. Unfortunately, if the results suggested elevated concentrations, the solution was more dispersion — not a reduction of emissions. I also worked for New Zealand’s electricity grid owner and operator. I was primarily carrying out cost-benefit analysis to ensure that New Zealanders were getting the best value for money for investments made to meet the nation’s upcoming electrification needs. This was important work but I did miss getting to flex my physics and mathematics research skills. Like many people with my background, I had a brief foray into finance.

AI Safety and Alignment Research

I’m now moving into AI safety and alignment research, because it’s a field where research-level mathematics can have a real-world impact. As AI systems become more capable, understanding what they have actually learned, and what they are actually optimising for, matters a great deal. There are open scientific questions about structure: what models represent, how, and whether we can verify output. My dispersion-modelling work left me with a clear picture of the failure mode alignment worries about: when the target is a measured quantity, the cheapest fix is often to optimise for the measurement rather than deal with the thing it was meant to track. I’d like to spend the next stage of my research career making sure the systems we build act in ways we actually intend.

Recently I completed the Iliad Intensive, a four-week, full-time course in foundational AI alignment research at the London Initiative for Safe AI (LISA).

I’m interested in how learned models represent concepts and world models, and in what mathematical structure can tell us about that. Questions I’m currently thinking about include:

  • what happens when a model has to pack more concepts than it has room to store independently (superposition), and whether that overlap explains why training in one area changes behaviour in another;
  • whether geometric and gauge-theoretic tools, such as holonomy, give useful invariants of learned representations;
  • how to describe AI systems precisely in terms of machines, preferences and world models.
