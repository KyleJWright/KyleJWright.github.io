Refinement in the Real World

Electromagnetism as a sequence of refinements, from two vector fields to differential cohomology, and what that might say about representations in machine learning.

A recurring move in physics is to notice that the object you were using was a shadow of a richer one. The richer object is not more decoration: it explains facts that the shadow can’t, and it often makes the shadow’s awkward features (frame dependence, unexplained integrality, quantities that should vanish but don’t) fall out automatically. Mathematicians call this kind of upgrade a refinement.

This post walks through one example that I find especially clean, electromagnetism, and then asks what the pattern might say about how we describe the internal representations of neural networks.

Averages forget

Start with something familiar. A moment of a distribution, such as the mean \(\mathbb{E}[X]\) or the variance \(\operatorname{Var}[X]\), is an average, and averages forget. Figure 1 shows three distributions with mean \(0\) and variance \(1\): a normal, a uniform, and a mixture of two narrow bumps. Knowing the first two moments only pins down the distribution if you already know it belongs to a two-parameter family such as the normals.

Figure 1. Three densities with mean 0 and variance 1. The first two moments can't tell them apart.

Adding more moments does not always rescue you either. The lognormal distribution has finite moments of every order, yet there are other distributions with exactly the same moments. The sequence of moments is a genuinely coarser invariant than the distribution.

Representations depend on context

The same issue arises when we summarise what a neural network represents. It is tempting to look for a single global linear direction for a concept and to average activations over a dataset to find it. But the geometry of activations can differ between contexts, for example between prose and code, and a single global average blurs them together.

Two recent papers take this seriously using the language of gauge theory and sheaves. Javidnia clusters the activations of a language model into local charts, fits transport maps between overlapping charts, and measures the obstructions to stitching them into one global description, including a holonomy-type obstruction around loops of charts. Grant and Wang build a cellular sheaf over a graph of paraphrases: each paraphrase carries its own representation, edges carry maps comparing them, and the global sections of the sheaf pick out what is consistent across all ways of saying the same thing, while the first cohomology measures what fails to glue.

The way I read this: paraphrasing behaves like a gauge transformation. Two paraphrases are different presentations of the same content, so the content should be what survives after identifying them. That is a categorical equivalence rather than an equality, and the natural way to assemble locally consistent data into global data is the passage from a presheaf to a sheaf. This framing is my own gloss rather than a claim either paper makes in exactly these words.

To see why the refinement matters, and not just the language, it helps to look at a case where the full story is known.

Electromagnetism as a sequence of refinements

Two fields become one form

Through the nineteenth century the electric and magnetic fields were gradually tied together. Ørsted (1820) saw a current deflect a compass needle, Faraday (1831) found that a changing magnetic field induces a current, Lenz (1834) fixed the sign, Maxwell unified the equations, and Hertz (1887–88) produced and detected the predicted waves.

Special relativity then shows that \(\mathbf{E}\) and \(\mathbf{B}\) are not separately meaningful. A charge at rest has a purely electric field; the same charge seen from a moving frame also has a magnetic field circling its direction of motion (Figure 2). Which part of the field you call electric and which magnetic depends on the observer.

Figure 2. A boost mixes E and B. The frame-independent object is the field strength F.

The frame-independent object is a single 2-form on spacetime \(M\), the field strength. Up to sign conventions,

\[F = \sum_i E_i \, dx^i \wedge dt + \tfrac{1}{2} \sum_{i,j,k} \epsilon_{ijk} B_i \, dx^j \wedge dx^k .\]

Maxwell’s equations become two lines. The homogeneous pair (no magnetic monopoles, and Faraday’s law) is

\[dF = 0,\]

and the pair with sources (Gauss’s law and the Ampère–Maxwell law) is

\[d{\star}F = J,\]

where \(\star\) is the Hodge star of the spacetime metric and \(J\) is the current 3-form. In vacuum, \(d{\star}F = 0\) as well, and on a compact Riemannian slice the pair \(dF = d{\star}F = 0\) says that \(F\) is harmonic, which is where Hodge theory enters.

Because \(dF = 0\), the field strength defines a de Rham class \([F] \in H^2(M;\mathbb{R})\). Integrating \(F\) over a closed surface measures the total magnetic flux leaving it, which is the magnetic charge enclosed. Locally, \(dF = 0\) also means \(F = dA\) for a potential 1-form \(A\), and the potential is only defined up to a gauge transformation \(A \mapsto A + d\lambda\), which leaves \(F\) unchanged.

So far the story is: two frame-dependent vector fields refine to one 2-form, and the 2-form carries a real cohomology class.

Charge is quantised

Millikan’s oil-drop experiment showed that electric charge comes in integer multiples of a basic unit. Dirac noticed that quantum mechanics links this to magnetism. If a particle of charge \(q\) moves in the field, its wavefunction must be single-valued, and that forces

\[\frac{q}{2\pi\hbar} \int_\Sigma F \in \mathbb{Z}\]

for every closed surface \(\Sigma\). In cohomological terms, the class \(\left[\tfrac{q}{2\pi\hbar}F\right]\) must lie in the image of \(H^2(M;\mathbb{Z}) \to H^2(M;\mathbb{R})\). A single magnetic monopole anywhere in the universe would therefore force all electric charges to be integer multiples of a basic unit.

The integral class is extra data. It is the first Chern class \(c_1\) of a complex line bundle on which the wavefunction lives, and it is not determined by \(F\): the map \(H^2(M;\mathbb{Z}) \to H^2(M;\mathbb{R})\) forgets torsion. So the naive object, a real cohomology class, is refined to an integral one.

The potential is measurable

Classically one regards \(A\) as a convenient fiction and \(F\) as the physical field. Aharonov and Bohm showed that this is wrong in quantum mechanics. Send electrons around both sides of a long shielded solenoid (Figure 3). Outside the solenoid \(F = 0\), so every naive invariant built from \(F\) vanishes where the electrons go. Yet the interference pattern shifts by the phase

\[\exp\!\left( \frac{iq}{\hbar} \oint_{\gamma_1 - \gamma_2} A \right) = \exp\!\left( \frac{iq\Phi}{\hbar} \right),\]

where \(\Phi\) is the flux inside the solenoid. The shift was observed by Chambers and, with the field completely shielded by a superconductor, by Tonomura and collaborators.

Figure 3. The Aharonov–Bohm set-up. The field strength vanishes on every path the electron can take, but the holonomy around the solenoid does not.

The region the electrons explore is space minus a cylinder, which deformation retracts onto a circle. Its second cohomology vanishes, so neither \([F]\) nor the Chern class can see anything. What is measured is the holonomy of \(A\) around the loop, a gauge-invariant number in \(U(1)\). Wu and Yang summarised the situation neatly: \(F\) underdescribes electromagnetism, \(A\) overdescribes it, and the phase factor describes it exactly.

The object that captures all of this is a line bundle with connection up to isomorphism, equivalently a Cheeger–Simons differential character or a class in degree-2 Deligne cohomology. Normalising so that \(F\) has integer periods, write \(\check{H}^2(M)\) for the group of these. It sits in two short exact sequences,

\[0 \to \Omega^1(M)/\Omega^1_{\mathbb{Z}}(M) \to \check{H}^2(M) \to H^2(M;\mathbb{Z}) \to 0,\] \[0 \to H^1(M;\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}) \to \check{H}^2(M) \to \Omega^2_{\mathbb{Z}}(M) \to 0,\]

where \(\Omega^k_{\mathbb{Z}}\) denotes closed forms with integer periods. The first sequence says a differential class is a topological class plus a potential modulo large gauge transformations. The second says it is a curvature plus flat data, and the flat part \(H^1(M;\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z})\) is precisely where the Aharonov–Bohm phase lives. Simons and Sullivan showed that these sequences, organised into the hexagon of Figure 4, characterise the theory uniquely.

Figure 4. The differential cohomology hexagon in degree 2. The diagonals through the centre are the two exact sequences above.

The moral: the naive invariant \(H^2(M;\mathbb{R})\) is not enough, and neither is \(H^2(M;\mathbb{Z})\). Both must be refined to differential cohomology before the theory accounts for what we can measure.

A limit and a colimit

One might hope to define the refined object by a commuting square: a differential class should be a curvature form together with an integral class whose images in real cohomology agree. That is the ordinary pullback of \(\Omega^2_{\mathrm{cl}}(M) \to H^2(M;\mathbb{R}) \leftarrow H^2(M;\mathbb{Z})\), and it is too coarse. The map from \(\check{H}^2(M)\) onto it has kernel \(H^1(M;\mathbb{R})/H^1(M;\mathbb{Z})\), the flat connections on a topologically trivial bundle. For the Aharonov–Bohm region this kernel is \(\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}\): the strict pullback loses exactly the effect we were trying to capture.

The fix is to remember not just that the two images agree but how. Hopkins and Singer define a differential cocycle as a triple \((c, h, \omega)\): an integral cochain \(c\), a closed form \(\omega\), and a real cochain \(h\) with \(\delta h = \omega - c\). The cochain \(h\) is a witness that the square commutes, and two cocycles are equivalent only if their witnesses are compatible too. This is a homotopy pullback (a homotopy limit), shown in Figure 5, and \(\check{H}^2(M)\) is its group of components.

Figure 5. Differential cohomology as a homotopy pullback. The square commutes only up to the homotopy h, and h is part of the data.

There is also a colimit description, which is closer to how a physicist thinks. On a single coordinate patch a gauge field is a 1-form \(A\), and gauge transformations \(g: U \to U(1)\) act by \(A \mapsto A + g^{-1}dg\). Rather than taking the quotient set, take the quotient groupoid, a homotopy quotient \(\Omega^1 /\!/ C^\infty(-,U(1))\). This is a presheaf of groupoids, and it does not yet know how to glue: on a sphere around a monopole there is no global potential at all. Imposing descent (stackification, the groupoid version of sheafification) produces the stack \(\mathbf{B}U(1)_\nabla\) of line bundles with connection, and \(\check{H}^2(M)\) is the set of isomorphism classes of its sections over \(M\). Bunke, Nikolaus and Völkl develop this point of view systematically using sheaves of spectra, and Freed, Moore and Segal show that the refinement has physical consequences for fluxes in string theory.

So the answer to the question “is there a (co)limit description?” is yes, twice: a homotopy limit that glues curvature to topology, and a homotopy colimit (a gauge quotient) followed by sheafification that glues local potentials into global ones. In both, the important step is keeping identifications as data instead of collapsing them to equalities.

Back to representations

The electromagnetic story suggests a checklist for describing what a model represents.

  • Averages are curvature-level data. A global mean direction, like \([F]\), is a coarse invariant. Two quite different internal geometries can share it.
  • Context charts and transports are connection-level data. Local descriptions plus the maps between them carry more information than any single global summary, in the same way that a connection carries more than its curvature.
  • Holonomy can be non-zero when local data look trivial. If you go around a loop of contexts and come back with a different representation, that is an obstruction no amount of averaging will detect. This is the analogue of the Aharonov–Bohm phase, and it is what the holonomy obstruction in Javidnia’s atlas is designed to find.
  • Equivalences should be kept as data. Paraphrases, like gauge transformations, should be glued with their identifications rather than quotiented away too early, which is the presheaf to sheaf step.

Whether trained networks actually have non-trivial holonomy in a meaningful sense is an empirical question, and I think a good one. Physics at least tells us what the right invariants look like, and that the naive ones can miss exactly the effects we care about.
