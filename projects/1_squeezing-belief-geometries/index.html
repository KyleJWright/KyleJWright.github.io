Squeezing belief geometries

Reproducing the belief-state simplex of Shai et al. in a small transformer, then squeezing it into a residual stream too narrow to hold it

This project starts by reproducing the belief-state simplex of Shai et al. (2024) in a small transformer. As an extension, I wanted to see what happens to belief states when the model is squeezed: when the residual stream is too narrow to hold one simplex, or must hold two processes with room for only one.

The short answer: the model keeps what its output needs and, as room runs out, gives up the rest, cheapest (in terms of next-token cross-entropy loss) first. The limit is set by what the output layer can read, not by how many directions fit in the space.

Note: Related questions (superposition, belief-state geometry) are active research topics; this project is a hands-on exploration of them in a setting small enough to compute everything exactly.

Code and full write-up: github.com/KyleJWright/squeezing-belief-geometries. The repository README has every table, and THEORY.md has the mathematics.

Background

While at the Iliad Intensive I was introduced to the paper Transformers Represent Belief State Geometry in their Residual Stream (Shai et al. 2024). The image of a hidden Markov model encoded in the network is striking. Seeing the belief-state simplex in the residual stream gives strong support to the idea that training learns models of beliefs about the hidden state, updated optimally, as predicted in theory (at least in some simple cases).

I wanted to get my hands dirty: to see whether I could reproduce these results, with the assistance of Claude, and then extend the code to ask some simple follow-up questions.

During the Intensive I was also introduced to a version of the superposition hypothesis of Elhage et al. (2022), which suggests that in deep networks it is typical for some linearly represented features not to be orthogonal. The cartoon picture is trying to squeeze more than \(n\) vectors into \(\mathbb{R}^n\): they can’t all be mutually orthogonal.

I’m interested in better understanding how this squeezing works in the setting of Shai et al.

Summary

The main finding. The model keeps what the next-token prediction needs and, as room runs out, gives up everything else, cheapest first. Here “cheapest” means costing the least next-token loss to give up. This is measured directly for the simultaneous case (section 3) and inferred from the loss numbers elsewhere. Each experiment shows a different piece of this:

  • Reproduction. A 4-layer transformer trained on the Mess3 process carries the fractal belief simplex in its final residual stream (\(R^2 = 1.00\) on held-out prefixes); shuffling beliefs before fitting gives \(R^2 = 0.00\).
  • One process, too narrow. Fine fractal detail goes first, at almost no cost in loss. The simplex survives down to width 4 and fails at width 3, where the decoded beliefs collapse onto a curve. The threshold matches the readout limit: after the final LayerNorm, the output head of a model of width \(d\) can produce a family of predictions with at most \(d-2\) parameters, and Mess3 needs 2.
  • Two processes simultaneously. As width falls, the model drops one process entirely, and it is the one whose memory is worth ~30 times less in loss. Dropping it costs almost exactly what that memory is worth (0.50% of the optimal loss).
  • Two processes, one at a time. Each sequence comes from one process, and both beliefs survive down to width 4. When the processes use separate tokens, their belief patches point in similar directions but sit apart. When they share the same three tokens, the patches lie on top of each other, with the same directions and the same centre, at every width. This holds even at width 8, where there is room to keep them apart. A likely reason is that either way the model must predict a distribution over the same three tokens, so one patch can serve both. Sharing costs nothing measurable: loss stays within 0.15% of optimal down to width 4, the same as with separate tokens.
  • Which process is running? With room to spare (width 8) the model also tracks which process is running, as well as an optimal observer can, in one extra direction: measured against the exact 6-state belief, it carries as much of it as the prediction plus the process posterior would (\(R^2\) 0.913 for both), never the full belief. Squeezed, that extra direction is the first thing to go: at width 4 the model learns it early in training and then gives it up.
  • Packing was not the constraint. I initially expected the Grassmannian packing problem to describe how the beliefs share the space. It mostly does not: when both beliefs are kept their planes are near-orthogonal, but the readout limit always binds first, and in the one-at-a-time case the problem is not a packing problem at all.
  • The planes are not flat. Measured with a Gauss map, every belief patch is a wrinkled sheet whose local tangent planes tilt 12–50° from its best-fit plane. Linear decoders never notice, because the wrinkles lie outside the plane. The planes are the linearly readable summary of curved patches.

Why this matters

Two lessons from this toy setting may be worth testing more widely.

  • Whether a latent variable is represented depends on room, not only on whether it is useful. The same training gives a model that tracks which process is running (width 8) and one that learns it briefly and then gives it up (width 4) while staying within 0.15% of the optimal loss, because the output does not need it. So a probe that finds nothing in a narrow model says little about whether the variable matters to the task or can be learned, and a signal found in a wide model need not survive in a narrower one. Methods that look for latent knowledge with probes need to allow for this.
  • What gets superposed or dropped is decided by the readout. The two behaviours of Elhage et al.’s toy models reappear: their dense features (here, processes running simultaneously) force the least valuable one out, and their sparse ones (one at a time) share directions. But here what decides is what the output needs and what each piece is worth, not how many vectors fit in the space. With separate tokens the one-at-a-time patches are told apart by an offset supplied by the token embeddings; with shared tokens they share one plane outright.

These are small models trained on a toy process; whether the lessons carry over is an open question.

Setup

Process. Mess3, the process used by Shai et al., is a hidden Markov model with 3 hidden states and 3 tokens, parametrised by \((x, \alpha)\). The belief \(\eta\) is the posterior over hidden states given the tokens seen so far; it lives in a triangle, and over all prefixes it traces out a fractal.

Model. A small transformer: 4 layers, each with one attention head (which lets a position draw on earlier tokens) and a small two-layer network (MLP, 256 hidden units). It reads up to 10 tokens. Each position carries a vector of \(d\) numbers, the residual stream. Every layer adds to it, and it is normalised by a LayerNorm before each read. Only the width \(d\) is varied; width 64 is the size used by Shai et al.

Training. Next-token prediction on freshly sampled sequences (256 per step, never repeated), with the Adam optimiser and a learning rate that decays from \(10^{-3}\): 20,000 steps for one process, 15,000 for two.

Simplex images. For every distinct prefix, take the final residual stream, before the last LayerNorm, fit an affine least-squares regression (with a small penalty) to the exact belief on half the prefixes, and plot the other half in the triangle, coloured by the true belief. Two numbers go with each image:

  • \(R^2\): the fraction of belief variance recovered on held-out prefixes.
  • Beyond last token: the last token alone predicts about 68% of a Mess3 belief, which gives three coarse blobs. This measures how much of the rest, the fine structure that needs memory of earlier tokens, the model captures: 1 is perfect, and 0 or below means no memory beyond the last token.

Two processes. Process A has \((x, \alpha) = (0.05, 0.85)\) and B has \((0.15, 0.60)\). In the one-at-a-time regime each sequence comes from one process, chosen at random. It comes in two versions: with separate tokens for each process, so every token reveals the process, and with one shared set of 3 tokens, so the model must infer the process from the statistics of what it has seen. In the simultaneous regime both run side by side, and each token is the pair of their tokens. In the language of Elhage et al. (2022), one at a time corresponds to sparse features and simultaneous to dense ones.

All numbers are means over 3 seeds; images are seed 0.

Width 8 keeps much of the fractal detail. Width 4 keeps the triangle and most of the memory, at almost no cost in loss. Width 3 breaks: the decoded beliefs collapse onto a one-dimensional curve through the triangle.

Why width 4. Every read of the residual stream goes through a LayerNorm, which removes the mean coordinate and then normalises to a sphere. So the output head sees a point on a sphere of dimension \(d - 2\), and the next-token distributions a model of width \(d\) can produce form a family with at most \(d - 2\) parameters. For Mess3 the optimal prediction is an invertible affine function of the belief, a 2-parameter family. If the model’s prediction varies smoothly with the belief, exact prediction then needs \(d \ge 4\). At \(d = 3\) the head sees a circle, a 1-parameter family, which matches the curve in the width-3 image. This is a heuristic, not a theorem: with a context of 10 there are only finitely many beliefs, and nothing forces the network’s map from prefixes to predictions to be smooth.

One at a time: both survive to width 4, with B losing detail first, and both fail at width 3, the same threshold as for one process. Only one process is active at a time, so the model never needs more than one 2-parameter family of predictions at once.

Simultaneous: one process is given up. B is already weak at width 6 and gone below it; the large negative values mean the decoder cannot even recover B’s last token, so B is dropped, not kept at low resolution. Two beliefs active at once need a 4-parameter family of predictions, so by the same heuristic exact prediction needs \(d \ge 6\); in practice width 6 is not quite enough.

Why B. B’s history is worth about 30 times less in next-token loss than A’s (0.002 against 0.061 nats for memory beyond the last token), so it is the cheap one to drop. Dropping B entirely costs 0.50% of the simultaneous optimal loss, and the width-4 simultaneous models sit at 0.50% above optimum: they predict A almost perfectly and B not at all.

With shared tokens the model cannot know it, especially early in a sequence, so even the Bayes-optimal predictor would not carry these beliefs exactly, and the ceiling is below 1.

4. Geometry of the belief patches

Each belief occupies a patch of the residual stream. Two questions about its shape: how do two processes’ patches sit relative to each other, and is each patch flat? Everything here is measured in the part of the stream LayerNorm can see, of dimension \(d - 1\).

The starting hypothesis was the cartoon of superposition: two beliefs squeezed into too little room should arrange their planes as an optimal Grassmannian packing, as far apart as the space allows. This turned out not to describe what the models do, for two reasons: the readout limit binds before packing ever does, and the patches are not flat planes but wrinkled sheets.

Overlap between processes

The overlap of two belief planes is the mean \(\cos^2\) of their principal angles: 0 for orthogonal planes, 1 for identical ones. Each plane is the best linear fit to its patch, so overlap measures how much two beliefs interfere for a linear reader, which is how every later layer first reads the stream.

Offset separation is the distance between the centres of the two processes’ patches, divided by their typical spread; random planes is the expected overlap of independent random planes, \(2/(d-1)\).

  • Simultaneous: near-orthogonal, but never constrained. Whenever both beliefs are kept (all seeds at widths 6 and 8) the planes are nearly orthogonal, overlap 0.01–0.02 against 0.3–0.4 for random planes. But orthogonality is easy there: it is possible from \(d \ge 5\), and the readout limit \(d \ge 6\) is reached first. At width 5 only one seed keeps B at all (overlap 0.26). Near-orthogonality may also simply be inherited from the optimal logits, whose A and B parts lie in orthogonal subspaces; the data cannot tell these apart.
  • One at a time, separate tokens: an arrangement, not a packing. Overlaps are far above the optimum, yet both processes decode well and loss is within 0.14% of optimal: the patches share directions but sit about 6 spreads apart. That separation comes from the token embeddings, not from anything the model had to learn, as the shared-token models show.
  • One at a time, shared tokens: one plane for two beliefs. Without separate tokens the offset drops to 0.05–0.4 spreads, and the planes coincide (overlap 0.9–0.97) at every width, including width 8, where there is room for two. Whichever process is running, the prediction is a distribution over the same 3 tokens, and one plane carries it for both, at no measurable cost in loss. Section 5 asks whether the model also tracks which process is running.

These conclusions hold in the metric the output head’s LayerNorm imposes and with planes fitted on optimal logits instead of beliefs.

Curvature within a process: the Gauss map

A linear decoder cannot tell whether a patch is flat: a surface that bends into an extra direction decodes perfectly once that direction is projected away. So the bending is measured directly.

  • Gauss map. At 64 anchor beliefs spread over the triangle, a local affine fit over nearby beliefs gives the local tangent plane, a point of the same Grassmannian. The tilt is its angle from the global plane. A synthetic flat patch gives the tilt that finite neighbourhoods produce on their own: the flat floor.
  • Bending. The average activation at each belief minus the global fit, split into the part outside the plane (bending) and inside it (a reparametrisation of the plane, which is not curvature), as a fraction of the variance the global fit explains.

(Excess tilt is the mean tilt above the flat floor, which is about 2–7°. Bending is undefined for simultaneous models of width 5 or less, where the two planes fill the readable space.)

  • No patch is flat. Local tangent planes tilt 12–50° beyond the flat floor, in every model.
  • Wrinkled, not bowed. Quadratic terms in the belief capture only a few percent of the activation variance, far less than the total bending: the patches are corrugated at fine scales rather than curved like a bowl.
  • Most bent at the corners. In most maps, tilt is largest near the vertices and edges, where beliefs are most confident, and smallest in the middle.
  • Shared tokens bend most. One possible reason, not tested: with shared tokens the model also tracks which process is running (section 5), and that coordinate may appear here as bending out of the shared plane.
  • This is much of what the linear fits miss. The global affine fit explains between about 40% and 97% of the activation variance, depending on the model, and this bending accounts for roughly a quarter to four-fifths of what it misses.

So the planes are the linearly readable summary of curved patches: each belief uses more than two dimensions, and the extra ones carry wrinkles that a linear reader never sees. The packing of planes describes linear interference between beliefs, not their full geometry. A caveat: with a context of 10 there are finitely many beliefs, so the “surface” is an average over nearby beliefs, and the finest wrinkles cannot be told apart from scatter.

5. Disentangling: does the model know which process it is in?

With shared tokens nothing tells the model which process produced a sequence. The exact posterior \(\pi = P(A \mid \text{prefix})\) says how far the evidence has gone, and it goes less far than one might expect: guessing the process from \(\pi\) is right only 62% of the time after two tokens and 81% after ten. The full belief therefore lives in the 5-simplex over (process, state), the join of the two triangles, and \(\pi\) says where along the join it sits.

What to expect. Shai et al. find that the residual stream carries the full belief state, not just the next-token prediction. Here that is the 5-dimensional joint belief, with coordinates \((\pi, \eta_A, \eta_B)\). The output, by contrast, needs only the 2-dimensional prediction \(\pi\, \eta_A N_A + (1 - \pi)\, \eta_B N_B\), in which \(\pi\) is already folded. So the final layer could hold anything from the 2-dimensional prediction up to the full 5-dimensional belief. And \(\pi\) competes with the prediction for the \(d - 2\) dimensions the output head can use, where the prediction is worth far more.

Does it track the process?

A ridge probe, an affine least-squares regression with a small penalty on the size of its coefficients (chosen on held-out data), is fitted from the residual stream to \(\pi\) at every layer, on the shared-token models of section 3.

At width 8 the model tracks the process essentially as well as possible: its probe follows the Bayes-optimal accuracy at every position, and the posterior is built up through the layers. Combined with section 4, the final layer holds one shared belief plane plus a separate direction for \(\pi\), as far as linear probes can tell: nearer the low-dimensional end, and not two separate belief models.

As the stream narrows, process tracking is the first thing to go. Loss stays within 0.15% of optimal down to width 4, but the probe falls to \(R^2 = 0.12\) and its accuracy stalls after a few tokens. This fits the readout limit of section 2: the output head has \(d - 2\) dimensions, the prediction needs 2, and only what is left can carry \(\pi\). At width 4 nothing is left. Dropping \(\pi\) costs little: the optimal prediction already mixes the two processes’ predictions in proportion to \(\pi\), so the posterior is folded into the prediction.

Does training learn one model and then split it?

Several settings learn a merged description first and split it later (causal-state splitting, deterministic annealing, stagewise learning in deep networks), which suggests a hypothesis: the shared-token model first learns one averaged process, then splits into two. Shared-token models at widths 8 and 4 (3 seeds each) were retrained with 19 snapshots between steps 25 and 15,000, and every snapshot was analysed in the same way.

The hypothesis is not borne out: the network never splits into two independent models.

  • Width 8: one model gains a coordinate. The probe for \(\pi\) rises steadily over training, and the offset between the A and B patches grows with it: the activations shift along a direction that tracks the process. The two belief planes stay nearly identical throughout, apart from a brief separation while loss is falling fastest, which reverses.
  • Width 4: learned, then given up. The probe rises to about 0.2 by step 600 and then falls back to 0.12, while loss keeps improving. The model tracks the process early and lets it go as it sharpens the prediction, which fills its two usable dimensions. A feature is learned and then squeezed out by a more valuable one.
  • No sharp transition. Loss falls smoothly at this snapshot resolution; nothing looks like a sudden phase change.

Comparison with two theoretical references

The shared-token data is one hidden Markov model with 6 hidden states, (process, state), so two exact reference points can be computed.

  • The joint belief. The exact posterior over the 6 states is the best possible belief state. A linear probe from the residual stream to it measures how much of it a model carries. To read that number, the same probe is fitted from idealised representations that hold only part of it: the next-token prediction alone, and the prediction plus \(\pi\).
  • The best single averaged model. A single 3-state hidden Markov model fitted to the mixed data by maximum likelihood (Baum–Welch). Its loss is what it costs to treat the two processes as one.

(Final layer, mean over 3 seeds.)

  • Width decides how much of the joint belief is kept. At width 4 the model carries about what the prediction alone carries; at width 8 it matches the prediction plus \(\pi\); widths 5 and 6 lie in between. No width carries the full joint belief, which would give \(R^2\) near 1. The final layer holds a projection of the joint belief that grows from the prediction to the prediction plus \(\pi\) as room allows.
  • No averaged stage. The best single 3-state model is 0.80% above the optimum (using only the last token: 4.1%; a uniform guess: 13.2%). Training passes through that level without pausing: width 8 drops below it by step 300, width 4 by step 800, with no plateau. There is no phase in which the network behaves like one averaged process, consistent with it never splitting into two.

Limitations

  • Width changes more than storage. Shrinking the residual width shrinks the space the belief is stored in, but also the space the network computes the belief update in. A failure at width 3 could come from storage, computation or optimisation; the large spread between seeds at width 3 suggests optimisation plays a part.
  • The readout limit is a heuristic at finite context. It is a theorem only in the long-context limit, and depends on the fractal’s Hausdorff dimension, which is not computed here.
  • Linear probes only. At small widths a belief may still be present but encoded nonlinearly, which a linear probe would miss. This matters for the link to superposition, which is about features recovered nonlinearly despite interference.
  • Training dynamics are sampled coarsely. The development run has 19 snapshots, 3 seeds and two widths; a brief transition between snapshots would be missed.
  • Curvature is measured on averages. The Gauss map and bending use averages over nearby beliefs and a finite neighbourhood, so wrinkles finer than the neighbourhood are not resolved.
  • The packing comparison is never constrained. Simultaneous models keep both beliefs only where orthogonal planes are possible, and their near-orthogonality may be inherited from the structure of the optimal logits.
  • One process pair. A and B differ a lot in how much their memory is worth, which decides which one is dropped. A symmetric pair would test whether the model then shares the cost or still picks one.
  • Images are sensitive to cheap structure. Width 8 loses visible fractal detail at no measurable cost in loss. The pictures magnify structure that is worth very little to the loss.
  • Small scale. Three seeds, one architecture, fixed training lengths, final layer only. The simultaneous comparison with packing rests on 7 models that keep both beliefs.
