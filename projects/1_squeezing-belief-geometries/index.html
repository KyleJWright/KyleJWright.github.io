Squeezing belief geometries

Reproducing the belief-state simplex of Shai et al. in a small transformer, then squeezing it into a residual stream too narrow to hold it

Reproducing the belief-state simplex of Shai et al. (2024) in a small transformer, then squeezing it: what happens to the simplex when the residual stream is too narrow to hold it, and when two processes must share a stream with room for one but not both?

The short answer: the model keeps what its output needs and, as room runs out, gives up the rest, cheapest first. The limit is set by what the output layer can read, not by how many directions fit in the space.

Code and full write-up: github.com/KyleJWright/squeezing-belief-geometries. The repository README has every table, and THEORY.md has the mathematics.

Background

While at the Iliad Intensive I was introduced to the paper Transformers Represent Belief State Geometry in their Residual Stream (Shai et al. 2024). The image of a hidden Markov model encoded in the network is striking. Seeing the belief-state simplex in the residual stream gives strong support to the idea that training learns models of beliefs about the hidden state, updated optimally, as predicted in theory (at least in some simple cases).

I wanted to get my hands dirty: to see whether I could reproduce these results, with the assistance of Claude, and then extend the code to ask some simple follow-up questions.

During the Intensive I was also introduced to a version of the superposition hypothesis of Elhage et al. (2022), which suggests that in deep networks it is typical for some linearly represented features not to be orthogonal. The cartoon picture is trying to squeeze more than \(n\) vectors into \(\mathbb{R}^n\): they can’t all be mutually orthogonal.

I’m interested in better understanding how this squeezing works in the setting of Shai et al.

Acknowledgements. I set the questions and the direction, ran every experiment on my laptop, and checked the mathematics. The code and first drafts of the text and derivations were written with Claude (Anthropic).

Summary

The main finding. The model keeps what the next-token prediction needs and, as room runs out, gives up everything else, cheapest first. Each experiment shows a different piece of this:

  • Reproduction. A 4-layer transformer trained on the Mess3 process carries the fractal belief simplex in its final residual stream (\(R^2 = 1.00\) on held-out prefixes); shuffling beliefs before fitting gives \(R^2 = 0.00\).
  • One process, too narrow. Fine fractal detail goes first, at almost no cost in loss. The simplex survives down to width 4 and fails at width 3, where the decoded beliefs collapse onto a curve. The threshold matches what the output layer can read: after LayerNorm, a model of width \(d\) can produce a family of predictions with at most \(d-2\) parameters, and Mess3 needs 2.
  • Two processes at once (dense). As width falls, the model drops one process entirely, and it is the one whose memory is worth ~30 times less in loss. Dropping it costs almost exactly what that memory is worth (0.50% of the optimal loss).
  • Two processes, one at a time (sparse). Both survive to width 4. With one shared set of tokens the two beliefs collapse onto a single plane at every width, even with room for two, because the output needs only one prediction, and it costs nothing measurable.
  • Which process is running? With room to spare (width 8) the model also tracks which process is running, as well as an optimal observer can, in one extra direction. Squeezed, that is the first thing to go: at width 4 the model learns it early in training and then gives it up.
  • Packing was the wrong lens. I expected the Grassmannian packing problem to describe how the beliefs share the space. It mostly does not: when both beliefs are kept their planes are near-orthogonal, but the readout limit always binds first, and in the sparse case the problem is not a packing problem at all.

Why this matters

Two lessons from this toy setting may be worth testing more widely.

  • Whether a latent variable is represented depends on room, not only on whether it is useful. The same training gives a model that tracks which process is running (width 8) and one that learns it briefly and then gives it up (width 4) while staying within 0.15% of the optimal loss, because the output does not need it. So a probe that finds nothing in a narrow model says little about whether the variable matters to the task or can be learned, and a signal found in a wide model need not survive in a narrower one. Methods that look for latent knowledge with probes need to allow for this.
  • What gets superposed or dropped is decided by the readout. The two behaviours of Elhage et al.’s toy models reappear: dense features force the least valuable one out, sparse ones share directions. But here what decides is what the output needs and what each piece is worth, not how many vectors fit in the space.

These are small models trained on a toy process; whether the lessons carry over is an open question.

Setup

Process. Mess3, the process used by Shai et al., is a hidden Markov model with 3 hidden states and 3 tokens, parametrised by \((x, \alpha)\). The belief \(\eta\) is the posterior over hidden states given the tokens seen so far; it lives in a triangle, and over all prefixes it traces out a fractal.

Model. A pre-LayerNorm transformer with 4 layers, one attention head and context length 10, trained on freshly sampled sequences. Only the residual width \(d\) is varied; width 64 is the size used by Shai et al.

Simplex images. For every distinct prefix, take the final residual stream, fit an affine map to the exact belief on half the prefixes, and plot the other half in the triangle, coloured by the true belief. Two numbers go with each image:

  • \(R^2\): the fraction of belief variance recovered on held-out prefixes.
  • Beyond last token: the last token alone predicts about 68% of a Mess3 belief, which gives three coarse blobs. This measures how much of the rest, the fine structure that needs memory of earlier tokens, the model captures: 1 is perfect, and 0 or below means no memory beyond the last token.

Two processes. Process A has \((x, \alpha) = (0.05, 0.85)\) and B has \((0.15, 0.60)\). In the sparse regime each sequence comes from one process, chosen at random. It comes in two versions: with separate tokens for each process, so every token reveals the process, and with one shared set of 3 tokens, so the model must infer the process from the statistics of what it has seen. In the dense regime both run side by side, and each token is the pair of their tokens.

All numbers are means over 3 seeds; images are seed 0.

Width 8 keeps much of the fractal detail. Width 4 keeps the triangle and most of the memory, at almost no cost in loss. Width 3 breaks: the decoded beliefs collapse onto a one-dimensional curve through the triangle.

Why width 4. Every read of the residual stream goes through a LayerNorm, which removes the mean coordinate and then normalises to a sphere. So the output head sees a point on a sphere of dimension \(d - 2\), and the next-token distributions a model of width \(d\) can produce form a family with at most \(d - 2\) parameters. For Mess3 the optimal prediction is an invertible affine function of the belief, a 2-parameter family. If the model’s prediction varies smoothly with the belief, exact prediction then needs \(d \ge 4\). At \(d = 3\) the head sees a circle, a 1-parameter family, which matches the curve in the width-3 image. This is a heuristic, not a theorem: with a context of 10 there are only finitely many beliefs, and nothing forces the network’s map from prefixes to predictions to be smooth.

Sparse: both survive to width 4, with B losing detail first, and both fail at width 3, the same threshold as for one process. Only one process is active at a time, so the model never needs more than one 2-parameter family of predictions at once.

Dense: one process is given up. Two beliefs active at once need a 4-parameter family of predictions, so by the same heuristic exact prediction needs \(d \ge 6\); in practice width 6 is not quite enough.

Why B. B’s history is worth about 30 times less in next-token loss than A’s (0.002 against 0.061 nats for memory beyond the last token), so it is the cheap one to drop. Dropping B entirely costs 0.50% of the dense optimal loss, and the width-4 dense models sit at 0.50% above optimum: they predict A almost perfectly and B not at all.

The scores are lower at the wider widths, but this does not mean the model is worse. Each process’s belief is computed as if the process were known. With shared tokens the model cannot know it, especially early in a sequence, so even the Bayes-optimal predictor would not carry these beliefs exactly, and the ceiling is below 1.

4. Geometry: is it a packing problem?

Each belief occupies a 2-dimensional plane in the residual stream: the directions the activation moves as that belief moves. The overlap of two planes is the mean \(\cos^2\) of their two principal angles (0 for orthogonal planes, 1 for identical ones). Planes are measured in the part of the stream that LayerNorm can see, which has dimension \(d - 1\).

The starting hypothesis was the cartoon of superposition: two beliefs squeezed into too little room should arrange their planes as an optimal Grassmannian packing, as far apart as the space allows. This turned out to be mostly the wrong lens. When both beliefs are kept, their planes are indeed near-orthogonal, but that happens only at widths where orthogonality is easy, and the readout limit binds before packing ever does. In the sparse case the problem is not a packing problem at all.

Offset separation is the distance between the centres of the two processes’ activation patches, divided by their typical spread.

Dense: near the optimal packing. Whenever both beliefs are kept, their planes are nearly orthogonal: overlap about 0.01–0.02, against 0.3–0.4 for random planes. Two planes fit orthogonally once \(d \ge 5\), so packing is not what limits the dense models: the readout bound \(d \ge 6\) is reached first. Two cautions. First, the comparison never reaches the regime where packing is constrained: no dense model keeps both beliefs at a width where orthogonal planes are impossible. Second, near-orthogonality has a simpler possible cause than packing pressure: the optimal logits for A and B lie in orthogonal subspaces of logit space, so planes the output head maps onto them through a reasonably well-conditioned map come out nearly orthogonal anyway. The data cannot tell these apart.

Sparse, separate tokens: not a packing problem. Overlaps are far above the optimum, and at width 4 above even the random baseline, yet both processes are decoded well and loss is within 0.14% of optimal. The two belief patches share directions but sit about 6 spreads apart, so position, not direction, tells them apart. Only one patch is active at a time, so nothing forces their directions apart.

Sparse, shared tokens: one plane for two beliefs. Remove the separate tokens and the separation goes with it: the offset drops from about 6 spreads to 0.05–0.4, so the 6 spreads came from the token embeddings, not from anything the model had to learn. The planes coincide too, with overlap 0.9–0.97 at every width, including width 8, where there is room for orthogonal planes and for a separate offset. The model is not squeezed into sharing; it chooses one plane for both beliefs, and loses nothing measurable by doing so (loss within 0.15% of optimal down to width 4).

A natural reading: whichever process is running, the next-token prediction is a distribution over the same 3 tokens, and one plane can carry it for both. This is consistent with the final layer holding mainly the next-token prediction, not two separate beliefs. The decoders here are told which process produced each sequence; section 5 asks whether the model itself tracks the process.

5. Disentangling: does the model know which process it is in?

With shared tokens nothing tells the model which process produced a sequence. The exact posterior \(\pi = P(A \mid \text{prefix})\) says how far the evidence has gone, and it goes less far than one might expect: guessing the process from \(\pi\) is right only 62% of the time after two tokens and 81% after ten. The full belief therefore lives in the 5-simplex over (process, state), the join of the two triangles, and \(\pi\) says where along the join it sits.

What to expect. Shai et al. find that the residual stream carries the full belief state, not just the next-token prediction. Here that is the 5-dimensional joint belief, with coordinates \((\pi, \eta_A, \eta_B)\). The output, by contrast, needs only the 2-dimensional prediction \(\pi\, \eta_A N_A + (1 - \pi)\, \eta_B N_B\), in which \(\pi\) is already folded. So the final layer could hold anything from the 2-dimensional prediction up to the full 5-dimensional belief. And \(\pi\) competes with the prediction for the \(d - 2\) dimensions the output head can use, where the prediction is worth far more.

At width 8 the model tracks the process essentially as well as possible: its probe follows the Bayes-optimal accuracy at every position, and the posterior is built up through the layers. Combined with section 4, the final layer holds one shared belief plane plus a separate direction for \(\pi\), as far as linear probes can tell: nearer the low-dimensional end, and not two separate belief models.

As the stream narrows, process tracking is the first thing to go. Loss stays within 0.15% of optimal down to width 4, but the probe falls to \(R^2 = 0.12\) and its accuracy stalls after a few tokens. This fits the readout argument of section 2: the output head has \(d - 2\) dimensions, the prediction needs 2, and only what is left can carry \(\pi\). At width 4 nothing is left. Dropping \(\pi\) costs little: the optimal prediction already mixes the two processes’ predictions in proportion to \(\pi\), so the posterior is folded into the prediction.

Does training learn one model and then split it?

Several settings learn a merged description first and split it later (causal-state splitting, deterministic annealing, stagewise learning in deep networks), which suggests a hypothesis: the shared-token model first learns one averaged process, then splits into two. Shared-token models at widths 8 and 4 were retrained with snapshots through training, and every snapshot was analysed in the same way.

The hypothesis is not borne out: the network never splits into two independent models.

  • Width 8: one model gains a coordinate. The probe for \(\pi\) rises steadily over training, and the offset between the A and B patches grows with it: the activations shift along a direction that tracks the process. The two belief planes stay nearly identical throughout, apart from a brief separation while loss is falling fastest, which reverses.
  • Width 4: learned, then given up. The probe rises to about 0.2 by step 600 and then falls back to 0.12, while loss keeps improving. The model tracks the process early and lets it go as it sharpens the prediction, which fills its two usable dimensions. A feature is learned and then squeezed out by a more valuable one.
  • No sharp transition. Loss falls smoothly at this snapshot resolution; nothing looks like a sudden phase change.

Limitations

  • Width changes more than storage. Shrinking the residual width shrinks the space the belief is stored in, but also the space the network computes the belief update in. A failure at width 3 could come from storage, computation or optimisation; the large spread between seeds at width 3 suggests optimisation plays a part.
  • The readout bound is a heuristic at finite context. It is a theorem only in the long-context limit, and depends on the fractal’s Hausdorff dimension, which is not computed here.
  • Linear probes only. At small widths a belief may still be present but encoded nonlinearly, which a linear probe would miss. This matters for the link to superposition, which is about features recovered nonlinearly despite interference.
  • Training dynamics are sampled coarsely. The development run has 3 seeds, two widths and a limited number of snapshots; a brief transition between snapshots would be missed.
  • The packing comparison is never constrained. Dense models keep both beliefs only where orthogonal planes are possible, and their near-orthogonality may be inherited from the structure of the optimal logits.
  • One process pair. A and B differ a lot in how much their memory is worth, which decides which one is dropped. A symmetric pair would test whether the model then shares the cost or still picks one.
  • Images are sensitive to cheap structure. Width 8 loses visible fractal detail at no measurable cost in loss. The pictures magnify structure that is worth very little to the loss.
  • Small scale. Three seeds, one architecture, fixed training lengths, final layer only. The dense comparison with packing rests on 7 models that keep both beliefs.
